Read this first

The outcome experiments use simulated people whose behaviour we defined. They show whether the mechanism works under stated assumptions. They are not evidence about real people; only a user study can be.

Do the films fit the mood, and are they good?

We asked for five films for each of 300 random moods and compared four ways of choosing them. "Mood-fit error" is how far, on average, the films' feel is from the target mood (0 would be perfect).

  • Vector search alone captures only about a third of the mood fit and keeps returning the same 50 films. The re-rank supplies the precision.
  • Our first version fitted moods well but recommended films of exactly average quality. Adding a quality floor gave up a little mood fit for much better and more varied films.
  • The index's answers match exact search (0.99–1.00 overlap in the top five, including filtered queries) and are up to 11× faster (1.3 ms vs. 14.5 ms).

60 simulated people for 40 days, three random seeds. "Mood error" is how far our estimate was from the true mood; "gain" is how much better the person felt afterwards (higher is better).

  • The quiz is the main sensor. Knowing the mood is worth about +0.08 gain over guessing from context.
  • Typing is a weak helper: it lowers mood error by about 7%. It is not a mood detector.
  • The adaptive quiz asks 18% fewer questions for 6% more error. The fused system asks 43% fewer for 9% more error.
  • In a separate test, adaptive question choice beat random choice at equal length (error 0.527 vs. 0.637).
  • Inconclusive: borrowing the population's experience for new users (first-week gain +0.184 ± 0.008 vs. +0.169 ± 0.039) and the people-like-you boost (+0.201 ± 0.016 vs. +0.189 ± 0.010) have overlapping intervals.

Match or change: a negative result, and what it taught us

First attempt

The simple version, "always change the mood", beat our learned policy (+0.266 vs. +0.210), and the learned policy picked "match" about equally often for every kind of person (24%, 26%, 29%). It was not adapting at all.

Why it failed (two reasons)

1. Nothing to learn. In our first simulation, changing the mood beat matching for every kind of person, even those who benefit from catharsis (+0.45 vs. +0.23). With no real differences between people, exploring was pure cost.

2. Too little data. Each person's scorecard got about 5 observations in 40 days, far too few to tell strategies apart.

What we changed

We pooled a person's evidence across their moods, made the shared prior stronger (12 imaginary observations), and required a margin before leaving the default strategy. We also added a second scenario where being cheered up while low feels dismissive to a "cathartic" minority. Settings were chosen on a separate trial seed, then evaluated on three fresh ones, with every policy facing the same simulated people.

What this means

With these effect sizes, "change the mood by default and adapt cautiously" is the right product, and a self-tuning policy is a safeguard for the minority who are hurt by being cheered up, not a big win. Whether real people differ more than our strongest scenario is the open question. Only real data can answer it, and a 10-day pilot is too short to settle it.

Constant updates: what a live mood index costs

Every reading rewrites a person's live mood vector. We replaced 10% of 50,000 vectors per round, eight rounds, while the population drifted toward the query region.

Design consequence. The read-heavy film index is HNSW with iterative scan. For the live mood index HNSW is fine at pilot scale (one write per reading) but would need batched writes or periodic rebuilds at scale.

What could be wrong

  • Simulated people. All outcome results use synthetic users whose responses we defined. The effect sizes are assumptions; the "strong differences" scenario was constructed to bracket the plausible range, not measured.
  • Tuning. Policy settings (prior strength 12, pooling 1.0, margin 0.08) were chosen on one trial seed and evaluated on three others. The quality floor, pool size and slate temperature were tuned on random moods over the same catalogue.
  • The feel scores are hand-made, and "better" is defined as more pleasant and less extreme. Both are design choices, not facts.
  • Typing parameters are not calibrated. We found no public typing data labelled with emotion, so the starting values come from published findings.
  • Benchmarks use synthetic scale on one machine.
  • The sensing results were produced before the final policy was fixed; the policy changes do not touch sensing.
  • No pilot yet. A protocol, consent sheet and hypotheses written in advance are ready, and the study needs instructor approval first.
