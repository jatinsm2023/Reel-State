How it works

Reel State guesses how you feel, finds films that suit that feeling, and then asks how you feel afterwards, so it can learn whether matching your mood or changing it works better for you.

Everything on this page is what the code actually does. Numbers and settings are the real ones.

The idea in one picture

Most recommenders learn what you like. This one also asks how you feel right now, because the same person wants different films on a tired Tuesday and a happy Saturday. After each film it checks whether the choice helped, and that is the only place real feedback enters the system.

Words you will see

WordPlain meaning
ValenceHow pleasant a feeling is, from unpleasant (−1) to pleasant (+1). Also used for films: how pleasant they feel.
ArousalHow much energy is in the feeling, from calm (−1) to intense (+1). A film can be calm or intense in the same way.
Mood beliefOur best guess of your valence and arousal plus how unsure we are. The doubt matters: it decides when to ask you a question.
EmbeddingA list of 384 numbers that describes a film (or a mood) so that similar things have similar numbers.
Vector search / ANNFinding the films whose numbers are closest to the mood's numbers without checking every film. The index that does this is called HNSW.
Match / change (regulate)Two strategies. Match: films that feel like your mood. Change: films that move you toward pleasant and the opposite energy.
PolicyThe rule that chooses match or change for you. It learns from your check-ins.

What happens in one session

This is a real sequence, so the numbers mean order.

  1. You arrive. The app notes the hour and weekday. This is a very weak hint about mood (afternoons are livelier than 4 a.m.).
  2. You write a few lines. Your words stay in the browser. Only the rhythm (gaps between keystrokes and a coarse key type) is sent. See Reading your mood.
  3. The app decides whether to ask. If yesterday's mood has faded and the typing was not informative, it asks one to six quick questions. If it already knows you well enough, it asks nothing.
  4. It updates your mood belief and saves it as a new row in the database (the history is never overwritten).
  5. The policy picks a strategy for your kind of mood: match or change.
  6. One query finds films near the target mood, respecting your filters and skipping films you were already shown.
  7. You watch and tap how you feel. The system scores whether the film helped and updates the policy and your mood belief.

A film is kept if it has Tag Genome scores and at least 50 ratings. Each film is stored with its 384-number embedding, its valence and arousal, its strongest 30 tags, and its rating statistics.

Where each piece of data lives

Reading your mood

Three signals feed one belief. They differ a lot in how much to trust them.

SignalHow reliableWhat it gives
Quick questionsstrongAn explicit answer about mood and energy. In our simulations this is the main sensor.
How you typeweakFaster typing hints at higher energy; more corrections and pauses hint at a lower mood. Compared with your own usual rhythm, not with other people.
Time of dayvery weakEnergy peaks mid-afternoon (4 p.m.) and dips at night; weekends are slightly happier.

Typing: what leaves your browser

Each of the five numbers is turned into a z-score against your own history (your first sessions borrow from a typical typist, then blend toward you). The mood evidence from typing is deliberately given large uncertainty, so it nudges the belief without ever deciding it.

The adaptive quiz

The quiz is not a fixed form. It has a bank of 20 questions (10 about how pleasant you feel, 10 about energy), each with five answers. After every answer the app recomputes what it believes, then picks the question that would shrink its uncertainty the most. It stops as soon as it is sure enough.

Putting the signals together

Your mood is treated as a hidden state that drifts, and each signal as a noisy look at it. After a good observation we know your mood well; as time passes, knowledge fades. About every six hours, half of what we knew has gone. When our doubt grows past a threshold (0.75 on either axis), the app asks again; until then it stays quiet.

From mood to films

Giving every film a feel

Each film gets a valence and arousal from its strongest tags: for every tag in our 176-tag lexicon we add the tag's score, weighted by how strongly the film matches it (cubed, so strong matches dominate). Scores are then spread out across the whole range so films are not all bunched in the middle. Spot checks: Saw and Grave of the Fireflies land at the unpleasant end, Toy Story and Amélie at the pleasant end, Mad Max: Fury Road at the top for intensity. The lexicon is hand-made and debatable in places (it rates The Shawshank Redemption as pleasant), which is why it sits in one table that can be replaced.

Putting a mood in the same space as the films

A film is a list of 384 numbers built from its title, genres, plot and strongest tags by a small language model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2). To search with a mood we need a mood in the same space. We fit a smooth curve from (valence, arousal) to "what a typical film with that feel looks like" in those 384 numbers. Evaluating it for your target gives the search vector.

The search

Why not just rank by similarity?

We tried. Vector search alone fits the mood only roughly and keeps returning the same 50 films. Adding the "distance in feel" term fixes the fit; adding a quality floor stops it suggesting mediocre films. The Report shows the numbers.

Match or change?

Psychologists have long noted that people use films in two ways: some want content that mirrors a mood (a good cry), others want to be moved out of it (something light). Which is better differs between people, and between moods. Reel State treats that as a choice to be learned.

How it chooses

  • It keeps a scorecard for each person, for each of four kinds of mood (upbeat or low, wired or calm), and for each strategy.
  • Each scorecard is a Beta distribution. Each decision draws from the two scorecards (Thompson sampling), so a strategy that has barely been tried still gets a chance.
  • It leaves "change" only with evidence. Match is chosen only if its draw beats change by a margin of 0.08. This stops wasteful experimenting.
  • New users borrow. A new person starts from other people's average results (worth 12 imaginary observations), and what worked for you in other moods counts at full weight, because style tends to be a stable trait.

The reward

After a film you set two sliders and optionally tap "Loved it" or "Not for me".

reward = 0.6 × felt outcome + 0.4 × liked
where felt outcome = (rise in valence + 0.5 × drop in |arousal|) ÷ 1.5, rescaled to 0–1. "Better" therefore means more pleasant and less extreme. That is a design choice, stated openly.

Watching together

When several people share a screen, each has their own mood belief and their own strategy, which gives each a personal target. The targets are blended into one, with extra weight on people we are confident about and on whoever is feeling lowest (weight × 1.5 at the lowest mood). Films are ranked by the average distance to everyone's target plus a penalty on the worst-served person (0.30 × the largest distance), so nobody is sacrificed for the average. Every person then gets their own recommendation record and their own check-in, so each person's policy learns from their own reaction.

Honest note

In our simulations this fairness-aware blending did not beat simple averaging; all rules were within noise. It stays as a feature, not as a claimed result.

The database

PostgreSQL 17 with the pgvector extension. The design choices that matter:

  • History and "now" are separate. mood_state only ever grows (a trajectory). mood_current holds one live row per person and is rewritten on every reading. Its index therefore sees constant updates, which is exactly the load the benchmarks stress.
  • Retrieval is one statement that mixes relational filters with vector ordering:
SELECT movie_id, title, genres, runtime_min, valence, arousal, avg_rating,
       1 - (content_emb <=> :mood_vector) AS cosine
FROM   movies
WHERE  (:max_runtime IS NULL OR runtime_min <= :max_runtime)
  AND  (:language    IS NULL OR language = :language)
  AND  (:genres      IS NULL OR genres && :genres)
  AND  NOT (movie_id = ANY(:already_seen))
ORDER  BY content_emb <=> :mood_vector
LIMIT  400;
  • Filters can starve a vector index, so every connection turns on HNSW iterative scanning (hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order, ef_search = 100, max_scan_tuples = 20000). Without it, a selective filter returns almost nothing. The Report shows the measurements.
  • Eight migrations build the schema; the recommendations and check-ins tables are what turn a catalogue into a feedback loop.
